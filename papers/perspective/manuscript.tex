\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Key Points:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Generative AI tools can produce fluent papers whose citations are real and whose reasoning is still weak.
\item Reporting guidelines help authors report a study completely; they do not ask whether each step of reasoning holds.
\item A claim register records what each claim rests on and checks each step by its kind, with wording tied to support.
\item Each part of the register is used elsewhere; none of the approaches compared here combines them for academic papers.
\item Publishing a register can make reasoning traceable; whether it helps reviewers find weak steps is untested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}

Writing assistants based on large language models (LLMs) have spread
quickly: up to 22\% of computer science papers now show signs of LLM
editing, and such editing can be detected in every major discipline
% D4: AI-augmented writing (definition, accepted)
\citep{liang2025}.  In this paper ``generative AI tools'', or ``AI'' for
short, means these assistants.  When a human author falls short, the text
tends to show it, in awkward phrasing or gaps in coverage.  An
AI-assisted draft can read as polished and complete while it contains
errors that only checking would reveal.

% S1-1: Citation hallucination (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
The most discussed of these errors are citation errors: a reference to
a work that does not exist, or to a real work with the wrong details.
\citet{mugaanyi2024} found that 62--89\% of the digital object
identifiers (DOIs) that ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) generated for natural-science
and humanities topics were fabricated.  \citet{walters2023} found fabricated
citations among the references of generated literature reviews, with
fewer from the newer of the two models they tested.
% S1-8: Search did not make citations reliable in one benchmark (descriptive, P2, EMERGING)
A 2026 benchmark tested newer models that can search the web.  Asked
for the bibliographic entries of 931 named papers, three of them
(GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash) got only 50.9\% of the
entries fully right; most errors were in single fields, but some were
details of a different paper \citep[\S4]{rao2026bibtex}.

% S1-2: Confidence inflation (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
A second error cannot be caught by a lookup.  I call it \emph{confidence
inflation}: wording that expresses more certainty than the evidence
supports.  The evidence indicates that LLMs tested in 2023 and 2024 verbalised
high confidence even on questions they answered wrongly
\citep{xiong2024,zhou2024relying}.
Whether this carries over into academic prose is unsettled: in one
corpus of applied-linguistics abstracts, ChatGPT used boosters such as
``shows'' at twice the human rate, but also more hedges
\citep{eldakhs2025}.  The stakes are epistemic, because words such as
``demonstrates'' and ``suggests'' are meant to tell a reader how much
weight a claim can bear.

% S1-9: A claim can outrun correctly cited sources (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
A third error is harder to see, because each citation passes: the
sources are real and say what is attributed to them, but the claim
drawn from them says more.  This paper's own register first stated
that LLMs ``invent plausible-sounding papers'', in the present tense,
from two studies of ChatGPT models from 2023 and 2024.  Each study was
cited correctly; the step from them to all current LLMs was not
supported.  A reader caught it, not a citation check.  Checking the register's source-based claims against their sources' scope then found three more like it \citep{veen2026repo}.

% S1-7: Citation checks alone pass weak reasoning (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D9: Citation check (definition, own)
A citation check, as I use the term and as such checks are usually run, asks whether a cited work exists
and says what is attributed to it, so it catches the first error.  It
does not reach the second or the third (Section~4 addresses both): a claim can rest on correct
citations and still say more than they carry, so a fluent paper can
pass the check with its reasoning unchecked.

% S1-6: Detection is no substitute (descriptive, P2, EMERGING)
% D5: Detection vs verification (definition, own)
Detecting AI-written text does not fill that gap.  Detection asks who
wrote a text; verification asks whether it holds.  Detectors may now answer the first question well for pure cases: one 2025 working paper found that the best of three commercial detectors separated published human prose from fully generated text almost without error
\citep{jabarian2025}.  Text that a person directs and an AI drafts, as this paper was, was not
tested; a detector still gives a verdict on such text, but the verdict
concerns the drafting, not whether the text holds.  But journal guidance now accepts AI help that authors disclose: the ICMJE recommendations ask authors to declare AI
use and describe how they used it \citep{icmje2026}.  Once AI help is
allowed and declared, the question that remains is whether the text
holds.

\section{Reporting Guidelines}

% S2-1: EQUATOR (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
The most developed infrastructure for checking academic writing is
the EQUATOR Network's collection of about 700 reporting guidelines for
health research \citepalias{equator}.  Guidelines such as CONSORT for
randomised trials and PRISMA for systematic reviews \citep{page2021}
give authors a checklist to complete while they prepare a manuscript.  The GoodReports tool picks the checklist that fits a study type
\citep{struthers2021}.

% S2-5: Reporting guidelines aim at complete reporting (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
What these guidelines check is reporting.  Their developers define a
reporting guideline as ``a checklist, flow diagram, or explicit text to
guide authors in reporting a specific type of research''
\citep{moher2010}.  For CONSORT, the measured effect is on completeness: journals endorsing CONSORT reported more completely on 25
of 27 checklist items, significantly so for five \citep{turner2012}.
Incomplete reporting is a recognised source of research waste
\citep{glasziou2014}, so this is a real service.  However, the question
it answers is not whether the reasoning holds.  That question applies
to any paper, and matters most where a paper has little else to check.

% S2-3a: Gregor's five theory types (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S2-3b: Proposed theories have no earlier source (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D3: Non-empirical paper (definition, own)
\citet{gregor2006} distinguishes five types of theory in
information systems research, among them analytic theories (Type~I)
and theories for design and action (Type~V).  When a paper of either
type proposes a new theory, no earlier source states it, because the
proposal is the contribution.  I call papers whose main contribution is
reasoning \emph{non-empirical}: theoretical, conceptual, design-science
and perspective papers are examples.

% S2-4: The reasoning carries the contribution (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
In a non-empirical paper the reasoning carries the contribution.
\citet{toulmin2003} places the step from evidence to claim in the
warrant, and \citet[p.~491]{whetten1989} calls the reasoning of a theory
``the theoretical glue that welds the model together''.  A check that
stops at citations may therefore leave the paper's contribution unchecked.

% S2-2: No consensus checklist for step-by-step reasoning (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
Guidance for papers without new data exists, but it is scattered.
Conceptual papers lack ``commonly accepted templates''
\citep[p.~18]{jaakkola2020}, although Jaakkola proposes four.
Design-science researchers have reported no agreed guidance on
presenting their work \citep{gregor2013,dinter2015}, and validity in
that field ``is neither well-understood nor consistently established''
\citep{larsen2025}.  Software engineering's community standards ask
methodological papers for ``clear, valid arguments'' but give no
procedure for checking them \citep{ralph2020}.

To look further, I searched for a consensus-based checklist that checks
the reasoning of non-empirical papers step by step: whether each step
from evidence to claim holds.  On 16 October 2026 I searched the EQUATOR
library (709 titles) and four bibliographic sources (Europe PMC,
OpenAlex, Crossref and arXiv) with 28 queries, and I screened 701 titles alone.  I
found none.  The search did not cover Scopus, Web of Science or
humanities indexes, and a second screener has not yet checked
it.  The protocol, queries and screened candidates are public
\citep{veen2026repo}, so the search can be repeated.

\section{Related Approaches}

recorded with its grounds, and each kind of grounds gets its own check.
The step from grounds to claim is checked for relevance and sufficiency.
The wording of a claim is tied to the strength of its support.  Several
practices already have one or more of these parts (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).

% S1-4: Recent AI-writing checkers check evidence (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Recent tools for AI-assisted writing go beyond checking that references
exist.  One linter also checks causal language against the study design
\citep{samsonau2026}.  One workflow returns unsupported claims to the
author \citep{chen2026}, and a provenance discipline samples whether the
evidence suffices \citep{zhou2026}.  A checklist for cited assertions in
narrative reviews is in development \citep{vanra2026}.  As their authors describe them, each asks whether
evidence exists and supports a claim; none describes a check of whether
the step from grounds to claim holds for its readers.

The appraisal tools come closer to the reasoning.  The evidence-synthesis
organisation JBI has a consensus-based checklist for appraising expert
opinion; it asks ``Does the opinion
demonstrate a logically defended argument to support the conclusions
drawn?'' and refers reviewers to Toulmin's model
\citep{mcarthur2025,jbi2026}.  SANRA, a scale for narrative reviews,
scores scientific reasoning as one of its six items, and its authors
call it ``not a reporting guideline'' \citep{baethge2019}.  Reporting items for concept analysis have only recently been collected
\citep{nashwan2026}.  Both judge an argument as a whole.

% S4-6: Assurance cases link claims, arguments and evidence (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Two practices record the structure of an argument explicitly.  In safety engineering, an assurance case, or safety case, is ``a
documented body of evidence that provides a convincing and valid
argument that a system is adequately safe'' for its use
\citep{bishop1998}.  Its authors ``make an explicit
set of claims'', link them to the evidence through arguments, and ``make
clear the assumptions and judgements underlying the arguments''
\citep{bishop1998}.  The Goal Structuring Notation breaks claims into
sub-claims until each can be supported by evidence \citep{kelly2004},
and the claims--arguments--evidence approach defines its arguments as
``Toulmin's warrants'' and treats claims asserted without justification
as assumptions \citep{bloomfield2014}.

% S4-7: Micropublications and nanopublications record claims (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
In scholarly data publishing, nanopublications express a core
scientific statement with its context and provenance \citep{groth2010}.  Micropublications record a statement with its
attribution and, at their fullest, with the evidence and challenges
brought for and against it; their model ``is grounded in
Toulmin-Verheij'' \citep{clark2014}.  Both make claims and their support
machine-readable.  Neither describes a procedure for checking a step.

% S4-8: Argument-based validation (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Educational measurement has an example of checking an
argument step by step.  In Kane's argument-based approach, the claims
made from test scores are laid out as an argument that specifies the
inferences and assumptions they need.  Validation then evaluates the coherence and completeness of that argument and the plausibility of
its inferences and assumptions \citep{kane2013}.  The object is the use
of a test, not a paper.

% S4-5: Argument-quality research supplies criteria (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
Research on argument quality supplies criteria for the step itself.
\citet{wachsmuth2017} distinguish logical, rhetorical and dialectical
quality, and define a cogent argument as one with ``individually
acceptable premises that are relevant to the argument's conclusion and,
together, sufficient to draw the conclusion''.  \citet{walton2008}
collect argumentation schemes, which their publisher describes as
``forms of argument that capture stereotypical patterns of human
reasoning''.

% S4-9: The register combines what the approaches hold apart (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Each of the four parts therefore already has users
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}), but none of the approaches compared here
combines them for an academic paper.  That combination is what the next
section proposes.

\section{The Claim Register}

This paper proposes a claim register: a table, published with the
paper, with one row for each claim and one for the paper's
contribution.  Toulmin's layout of arguments gives each claim's row and
its step-by-step check; Whetten's account of a theoretical contribution
gives the contribution's row, checked once as a whole.  Box~1 gives the
terms; the framework's glossary defines them from their sources
\citep{veen2026repo}.

% S3-5: Toulmin's layout and its origin (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
Toulmin modelled his layout of arguments on jurisprudence, calling
logic ``generalised jurisprudence'' \citep[p.~7]{toulmin2003}.  In his account any assertion is a claim.  Its \emph{data} are ``the
facts we appeal to as a foundation for the claim''.  Its warrant is one
of the ``general, hypothetical statements, which can act as bridges''
from data to claim.  A \emph{qualifier} indicates ``the strength conferred by the
warrant on this step''; and a \emph{rebuttal} names ``circumstances in
which the general authority of the warrant would have to be set aside''
\citep[pp.~90--94]{toulmin2003}.  Toulmin later wrote that he had not
had in mind an analytical model like the one that came to be called
``the Toulmin model''; his concern ``was with twentieth-century
epistemology, not informal logic'' \citep[p.~vii]{toulmin2003}.  Using
his layout as a checklist is my adaptation, and it has to be judged on
its own merits.

% S3-1: Toulmin's elements used by others (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Others have put his elements to work.  Micropublications build on his
layout to record the claims of (mainly biomedical) publications
\citep{clark2014}.  Kane's argument-based validation analyses the
inferences from test scores to their uses with his model
\citep[pp.~11--12]{kane2013}.  Two studies of university students' essays
scored argument quality element by element, with two raters each
\citep{yang2022,divya2026}.    None of these uses checks a research paper's reasoning, so
that application is a further step.

\paragraph{Checks by kind of grounds.}
In the register, each claim is recorded with its grounds item by item,
and each kind of grounds gets its own check:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{source} is checked in two steps: the work exists, and it says what the claim attributes to it, for the cases and period the claim covers;
\item a \textbf{declared starting point} is checked for being stated,
  with a note on whether the field accepts or contests it;
\item the author's \textbf{own work}, such as a search or a trial, is
  checked for a method someone else could repeat;
\item a step from \textbf{other claims}, or from several sources to a
  point none of them makes alone, gets the warrant check.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The warrant check.}
The warrant check asks two questions, taken from Wachsmuth and
colleagues' definition of a cogent argument.  \emph{Relevance}: does
each ground bear on this claim, so that the claim would be less credible
without it?  \emph{Sufficiency}: do the grounds together justify the
claim at the strength its qualifier states?  A sufficiency check names
what is missing, such as a case not covered, a search not run or a
premise that rests on one study.  The check also records the rebuttal,
the circumstances in which the step would fail, and the strongest known
objection with the answer to it.  Where a step follows a common pattern,
such as an argument from expert opinion or from the absence of search
results, the questions proper to that pattern could be added.  I have not yet
worked them out from the argumentation-schemes literature.

The check has four outcomes: it passes, it passes and someone other than
the author confirms it, it leaves a named gap open, or it fails.  A
failed check means that the recorded grounds do not carry the claim as
worded, not that the claim is false; rewording the claim to what the
grounds carry is the usual response.

% S3-6: Calibrated language has precedents (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S3-7: Readers interpret fixed terms inconsistently (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% S3-3: Mapping tiers to wording (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D6: Tier-to-wording mapping is this paper's proposal (assumption, own)
\paragraph{Tiers and wording.}
Fixed wording for levels of certainty has precedents.  \citet{kent1964}
argued for ``a few key odds expressions'' in intelligence estimates.  The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) asks its authors to
judge confidence from the ``type, amount, quality, and consistency of
evidence'' and ``the degree of agreement'' \citep{mastrandrea2010}.  The GRADE working group (Grading of Recommendations Assessment,
Development and Evaluation) fixes wording per level of certainty and size
of effect, from ``results in'' to ``may result in'' \citep{santesso2020}.
Fixed terms may not secure a shared reading: when readers assigned
numbers to the probability terms in an IPCC report, their judgements
deviated from the IPCC's guidelines even when the guidelines were at
hand \citep{budescu2009}.

Table~\ref{tab:confidence} adapts the
practice for academic papers.  A claim's tier is the lower of two
ratings: how well its grounds are supported, and what its warrant check
allows.  An argued claim can therefore reach the Supported tier when its
premises are supported and its check passes, which a rule that only
counts sources does not allow.  The table has not been tested with
readers; it may help an author notice when a sentence claims more than
its grounds allow.

\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Support for the grounds} & \textbf{Warrant check} & \textbf{Qualifiers} \\
\midrule

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Priority.}
Every claim also carries a \emph{priority}, the author's answer to the
question of what fails if the claim is wrong.  A P0 claim is one the
paper fails without; P1 strengthens the argument; P2 gives context.
Priority is guidance on how much checking a claim deserves.  It is not a
gate: a paper whose central claims stay at Emerging is reporting how
far its evidence goes, and the register makes that visible.

% S3-2: Whetten as a check of the contribution (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
\paragraph{The contribution.}
Besides its claims, the register has one entry for what the paper
contributes, and that entry is checked differently: once, as a whole,
rather than step by step.  The check comes from Whetten.  He set out what ``a
complete theory must contain'': which factors (What), how they relate
(How), why (``the theoretical glue that welds the model together''), and
for whom, where and when it holds \citep[pp.~490--492]{whetten1989}.
His seven reviewer questions, from ``What's new?'' to ``Who cares?'',
answer ``What constitutes a publishable theory paper?''
\citep[p.~494]{whetten1989}.  They judge a contribution as a whole, so
they may serve as a check of a paper's central contribution, applied
once.  For a contribution that is a recommendation, they fit less well:
How tends to restate the thesis, and the check needs a declared value
and the conditions under which the recommendation applies.

% D8: Verification and validation mapping (assumption, own)
These two levels map onto a distinction from systems engineering.
Verification is ``proof of compliance with requirements''; validation
shows that a product ``accomplishes the intended purpose in the intended
environment'' \citep[\S2.0]{nasa2016}.  Checking each claim against its
grounds and warrant is verification in this sense; asking Whetten's
questions of the contribution is closer to validation.  The mapping is
mine.  The register then works as a traceability record, of the kind systems engineers keep for requirements.  Each link is
written down, from the contribution to its claims, from a claim to its
grounds and from grounds to their sources, so that someone else can
follow and check it.  The IPCC asks its authors for a ``traceable account'' of
their evaluation of evidence for the same reason \citep{mastrandrea2010}.

\paragraph{What passing means.}
A register that passes its checks means that the paper followed the
method: each claim's grounds and warrant were recorded and checked
against stated criteria.  It does not mean that the claims are true.  A
check run by the author, or by the author's AI tools, can err in the
author's favour, which is why only a check confirmed by someone else can
reach the Established tier.

% S3-4: Each claim needs the checks its grounds call for (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
The central argument of the proposal rests on these pieces.  A check
that rates an inference by counting its sources does not look at the step from grounds to claim.  It cannot tell a sound inference from an
unsound one, and it may fail a sound inference for the wrong reason.
Recording each claim's grounds item by item, and letting the kind of
grounds decide the check, may avoid such false failures.  The argument
rests on design reasoning and on two trial registries kept by the
framework's own tooling, and no such false failure has yet been documented in a paper
written by someone else.

\section{Applying the Register to This Paper}
\label{sec:self}

This paper was written with the procedure it proposes.  Its claim
register, published with the paper \citep{veen2026repo}, records each claim's grounds, warrant, warrant check, qualifier,
confidence tier and priority, plus the paper's declared starting points

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

% S4-3: Structure plus LLMs (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Its parts have users elsewhere (Section~3), but the register itself
has one so far: the author of this paper.  Whether it works in other
hands is untested.  Its
reviews came from five language models from four vendors, which read
the first two drafts in sessions separate from the drafting.  They are independent of the drafting but not of the method, and no substitute
for human reviewers.  Whether tools can carry part of the work is also
open.  Structured prompting was reported to improve LLM argument
extraction \citep{gupta2024}, and representing support and attack
formally to improve LLM review aggregation \citep{peerarg2024}.  Both
are narrow tasks, so they suggest only that structure combined with
LLMs may help with checking as well.

The register asks work of authors; this paper's has 36 entries.
A shorter form, the warrant check's questions for a reviewer to ask of
the central claims, may suit many papers better.  Toulmin's and
Whetten's frameworks come from Western academic traditions, and
scholars in the humanities may find a claim-by-claim layout foreign to
how their arguments work; their fit with other conventions needs to be
assessed.

The checks are also partly circular: the author who wrote the rules
applies them, and the warrant check is a judgement, recorded with its
reasons so that someone else can disagree with it.

% SECTION 7
% ===============================================================
\section{Recommendations}

% C1: A published register makes reasoning traceable; test it (contribution, P0, EMERGING)
% D1: Published reasoning should be checkable (value, accepted)
% D2: Verification infrastructure (definition, own)
% D7: A proposal should be tested before it is adopted (value, accepted)
The value behind this proposal is that published reasoning should be
checkable: readers and reviewers should be able to see what each claim
rests on.  A claim register, published with a paper whose contribution
is an argument, makes that reasoning visible and traceable.  I propose
two steps, in this order.  Authors and journals could publish registers
as optional supplementary material, as this paper does, and trials
should test whether reviewers who have a register find different
weaknesses from reviewers who do not.  A register should become part of
verification infrastructure for non-empirical papers, of the kind
EQUATOR offers for empirical health research, only if such trials find
that it helps.  A first implementation exists: templates for the
register and the glossary, the checks for each kind of grounds, and
tools that test a register against its manuscript (see Code and Data
Availability).

The proposal applies to papers whose contribution is an argument, and
to the interpretive parts of empirical papers.  Other writing that asks
readers to trust its reasoning, journalism among them, might use it too,
but that is untested.  Peer review already judges arguments, and a
register would not replace it.  It could show reviewers where a paper's
weight rests, and it could help authors find weak steps before
submission; whether it does either is the question the trials should
answer.

% S5-2: Next steps (normative, P2, SPECULATIVE)
Several further directions warrant investigation.  The gap search needs
a second screener and wider databases.  The questions proper to common
patterns of argument need working out, and the checks need adapting to
other fields.  The checks could also be built into writing tools, so
that they run while an author drafts.
