- **DOI:** [10.1111/bjet.13544](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544), checked against Crossref 2026-11-17

## Summary
A randomised experiment with 117 university students on a writing task. Four arms: ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools, no support. The ChatGPT group improved its essay scores most, but its knowledge gain and transfer did not differ significantly from the other groups, and its self-regulated learning processes differed in frequency and sequence.

## Key Findings (from the abstract)
- No difference in intrinsic motivation across arms.
- ChatGPT arm: best essay-score improvement, no significant difference in knowledge gain or transfer.
- The authors' interpretation: tools like ChatGPT "may promote learners' dependence on technology and potentially trigger metacognitive laziness". This is offered as a possibility, not measured as an outcome.

## Relevance to the framework
The term the Radboud ConfidAI project (Hasibi, NWO Vidi 2025) uses for its human-side problem. It bears on the reader's question already filed under L114–L115: does AI help that improves the product also reduce the checking the human does? Here the product (essay) got better while the learning showed no significant difference, which matches the framework's premise that a polished text is weak evidence that the writer verified it.

## Caveats (tier discipline)
- Peer-reviewed RCT → tier **A** once read in full; abstract only for now.
- Students learning to write, not researchers verifying claims. The transfer to scholarly writing is an analogy, not a finding.
- "Metacognitive laziness" is the authors' interpretive label, hedged in their own abstract ("may", "potentially"). Do not cite it as a demonstrated effect.
