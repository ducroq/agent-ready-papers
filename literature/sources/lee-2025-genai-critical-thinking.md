## Summary
An online survey of 319 knowledge workers, who shared 936 examples of using generative AI at work. Regression models relate task-specific and person-specific factors to the *perceived* enaction of critical thinking (Bloom's taxonomy) and the perceived effort it takes.

## Key Findings
- Abstract (p. 1): "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking."
- Abstract (p. 1): "GenAI shifts the nature of critical thinking toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship."
- p. 9: "knowledge workers' confidence in AI doing the tasks indeed negatively correlates with their enaction of critical thinking (β=-0.69, p < 0.001)"; confidence in doing the task oneself (β=0.26) and in evaluating AI responses (β=0.31) correlate positively.
- No main effect of task type on perceived critical thinking (p. 9).

## Caveats (tier discipline)
- Every measure is self-reported: perceived critical thinking, not observed. Cite it as "reported".
- Correlational: it shows an association with confidence, not that AI use causes less critical thinking.
- Knowledge workers in general, not academic authors: applying it to authors who keep a claim register is an inference.

## Relevance to agent-ready-papers
Raised by a reader's question on Paper 1: does a claim register replace part of the author's own thinking? The study supports both sides of that question. Trust in the tool goes with less reported critical thinking, while the critical thinking that is done moves towards verifying and integrating output, which is the kind of work a register asks for.
