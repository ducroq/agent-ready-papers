\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Key Points:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Generative AI tools can produce fluent papers whose citations are real and whose reasoning is still weak.
\item Reporting guidelines help authors report a study completely; they do not ask whether each step of reasoning holds.
\item A claim register records what each claim rests on and checks each step by its kind, with wording tied to support.
\item Each part of the register is used elsewhere, one of them only in part; none of the approaches compared here combines them for academic papers.
\item A register is a traceability record: authors keep it, and reviewers and readers can use it to follow each claim back to what it rests on.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}

% S2-2: No consensus checklist for step-by-step reasoning (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
Readers of a research paper rely on its sources and on the steps it
takes from them to its claims.  Citation checks look at the sources,
and reporting guidelines at whether a study is reported completely;
neither checks the steps.
% D3: Non-empirical paper (definition, own)
This paper is about \emph{non-empirical} papers: papers whose main
contribution is reasoning rather than new data, such as theoretical,
conceptual, design-science and perspective papers.  It asks whether
there is a consensus checklist for checking their steps, and if not,
whether we could build one.
A documented search found none (Section~2), so I propose a candidate,
a claim register, and apply it to this paper.

Writing assistants based on large language models (LLMs) have spread
quickly: up to 22\% of computer science papers now show signs of LLM
editing, and such editing can be detected in every major discipline
% D4: Generative AI tools and AI-assisted writing (definition, own)
\citep{liang2025}.  In this paper ``generative AI tools'', or ``AI'' for
short, means these assistants.  An
AI-assisted draft can read as polished and complete while it contains
errors that only checking would reveal.

% S1-1: Citation hallucination (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
The most discussed of these errors are citation errors: a reference to
a work that does not exist, or to a real work with the wrong details.
\citet{mugaanyi2024} found that 62--89\% of the digital object
identifiers (DOIs) that ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) generated for natural-science
and humanities topics were fabricated.  \citet{walters2023} found fabricated
citations among the references of generated literature reviews, with
fewer from the newer of the two models they tested.  Two later studies
also found invented references: about one in five citations from GPT-4o in 2025
\citep{linardon2025}, and 28\% of the references three models gave in
2026 with web search switched off, from 8\% to 50\% by model
\citep{seifi2026}, did not exist.

% S1-2: Confidence inflation (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
A second error is what I call \emph{confidence
inflation}: wording that expresses more certainty than the evidence
supports.  The evidence indicates that LLMs tested in 2023 and 2024 verbalised
high confidence even on questions they answered wrongly
\citep{xiong2024,zhou2024relying}.
Whether this carries over into academic prose is unsettled: in one
corpus of applied-linguistics abstracts, ChatGPT used boosters such as
``shows'' at twice the human rate, but also more hedges
\citep{eldakhs2025}.  This matters because words such as
``demonstrates'' and ``suggests'' are meant to tell a reader how much
weight a claim can bear.

% S1-9: A claim can outrun correctly cited sources (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
A third error is harder to see: the sources are real and report what
they are cited for, but the claim drawn from them says more than they
do.  A study that finds something for one group, period or setting is
cited as if it held for all of them.  A review of quotation-accuracy
studies in medical journals counts generalisation among citation errors,
though as a minor one \citep{mogull2017}, and most of ten LLMs asked to
summarise research papers generalised beyond them, more often than
human-written summaries did \citep{peters2025}.  Section~\ref{sec:register} works through an example.

% S1-7: Citation checks alone pass weak reasoning (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D9: Citation check (definition, own)
% S2-4: The reasoning carries the contribution (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% S2-5: Reporting guidelines aim at complete reporting (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
A citation check asks whether a cited work exists and says what is
attributed to it; a reporting guideline aims at complete reporting.
Neither asks whether the step from evidence to claim holds.  Peer
reviewers judge that step, but their judgement is usually not published
claim by claim, so a reader often cannot see which steps were checked, or against
what.  That matters most in a non-empirical paper, because there the step from evidence to claim is what the paper adds.

% S1-6: Detection is no substitute (descriptive, P2, EMERGING)
% D5: Detection vs verification (definition, own)
Detecting AI-written text does not fill that gap.  One 2025 working paper found that the best of three commercial detectors separated published human prose from fully generated text almost without error
\citep{jabarian2025}; the study left out text that a person directs and
an AI drafts.  Either way, a detector answers who drafted a text, not
whether it holds.  Journal guidance instead asks authors to declare AI use and describe how they used it \citep{icmje2026}.

% C1: A published register can make reasoning traceable; test it (contribution, P0, EMERGING)
% D3: Non-empirical paper; scholarly articles as the starting case (definition, own)
A claim register is a table, published with the paper, that records each claim
with what it rests on and how that was checked, so that each step can
be traced, as engineers trace requirements (Section~\ref{sec:register}).  Authors keep the
register; reviewers and readers can use it to see where a paper's weight
rests; journals decide whether to ask for one.  It builds on Toulmin's
layout of arguments and Whetten's account of a theoretical
contribution.  A consensus would need
others to test and revise it.  I start from non-empirical scholarly
articles, because the search in Section~2 covered
them and reporting guidelines offer a comparison.  Other writing that
asks readers to trust its reasoning, from student essays to journalism,
might use the register too (Section~\ref{sec:recs}).

\section{Reporting Guidelines}

% S2-1: EQUATOR (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
The EQUATOR Network collects about 700 reporting guidelines for
health research \citepalias{equator}.  Guidelines such as CONSORT for
randomised trials and PRISMA for systematic reviews \citep{page2021}
give authors a checklist to complete while they prepare a manuscript.  The GoodReports tool picks the checklist that fits a study type
\citep{struthers2021}.

% S2-5: Reporting guidelines aim at complete reporting (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
What these guidelines check is reporting.  Their developers define a
reporting guideline as ``a checklist, flow diagram, or explicit text to
guide authors in reporting a specific type of research''
\citep{moher2010}.  For CONSORT, the outcome measured is completeness: journals endorsing CONSORT reported more completely on 25
of 27 checklist items, significantly so for five \citep{turner2012}.
Incomplete reporting is a recognised source of research waste
\citep{glasziou2014}, so guidelines meet a real need.  Neither the definition nor that evaluation asks whether the reasoning holds.

% S2-3a: Gregor's five theory types (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S2-3b: Proposed theories have no earlier source (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D3: Non-empirical paper (definition, own)
\citet{gregor2006} distinguishes five types of theory in
information systems research, among them analytic theories (Type~I)
and theories for design and action (Type~V).  When a paper of either
type proposes a new theory, its parts may have earlier sources, but no
earlier source states the whole, because the proposal is the
contribution.  Such a paper is non-empirical in the sense of
Section~1 when its main contribution is the reasoning.

% S2-4: The reasoning carries the contribution: its grounds (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
In such a paper \citet{toulmin2003} places the step from evidence to
claim in the warrant, and \citet[p.~491]{whetten1989} calls the
reasoning of a theory ``the theoretical glue that welds the model
together''.

% S2-2: No consensus checklist for step-by-step reasoning (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
Guidance for non-empirical papers exists, but it is scattered.
Conceptual papers lack ``commonly accepted templates'', as Jaakkola
puts it, following an editorial by Cornelissen \citep[p.~18]{jaakkola2020}, although Jaakkola proposes four.
Design-science researchers have reported no agreed guidance on
presenting their work \citep{gregor2013,dinter2015}, and validity in
that field ``is neither well-understood nor consistently established''
\citep{larsen2025}.  Software engineering's community standards ask
methodological papers for ``clear, valid arguments'' but give no
procedure for checking them \citep{ralph2020}.

To look further, I searched for a consensus-based checklist that checks the reasoning of non-empirical papers step by step.  On 16 October 2026 I searched the EQUATOR
library (709 titles) and four bibliographic sources (Europe PMC,
OpenAlex, Crossref and arXiv) with 28 queries, and I screened 701 titles alone.  I
found none.  The search did not cover Scopus, Web of Science or
humanities indexes, and a second screener has not yet checked
it.  The protocol, queries and screened candidates are public
\citep{veen2026repo}.

\section{The Claim Register}
\label{sec:register}

% S3-5: Toulmin's layout and its origin (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
In Toulmin's layout any assertion is a claim that rests on grounds
(his \emph{data}) through a warrant, with a qualifier and conditions
under which it does not hold, which he calls the rebuttal and this paper
calls the claim's scope \citep[pp.~90--94]{toulmin2003}.  Toulmin's own
example: Harry was born in Bermuda (grounds), so, presumably
(qualifier), Harry is a British subject (claim), since a man born in
Bermuda will generally be a British subject (warrant), unless both his
parents were aliens (scope).  What supports the warrant itself, such as
the statutes behind it, Toulmin calls \emph{backing}; the register
records it only when readers may not accept the warrant.
\supplementref{} introduces the layout in more detail.  Toulmin later
wrote that he had not meant his layout as the analytical model that came to be
called ``the Toulmin model'' \citep[p.~vii]{toulmin2003}; using it as a checklist is an adaptation.

\paragraph{A worked example.}
Take the claim ``LLMs invent references''.  Its
grounds are two sources: \citet{mugaanyi2024} found that most DOIs
ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) generated were fabricated, and \citet{walters2023}
found fabricated citations in literature reviews generated by two
ChatGPT models.  The warrant is the rule that carries these findings to
the claim: what the models tested did, LLMs in general do.  The
qualifier is none: the claim is stated as plain fact, in the present
tense.  Its scope, as worded, is every LLM, now.

Both grounds are sources, so each gets the source check described
below.  Both works exist and report that the models they tested invented
references, so a citation check would pass the claim.  The source check also asks whether each
source says what the claim attributes to it, for the cases and period
the claim covers.  Neither covers that much: they tested ChatGPT models of 2023 and
2024, and the claim, as worded, covers every LLM, now.  The check fails.
Keeping the claim as worded would need an argument that other and later
models behave the same, and that step would get the warrant check.  The
usual response is to reword the claim to what the sources carry: ``LLMs tested in 2023 and 2024 invented
references''.  Now the scope matches the sources, the check passes, and
two independent peer-reviewed sources put the claim at the Supported
tier (Table~\ref{tab:confidence}), which permits ``indicates'' or a
plain report of what the studies found.  The other response is to find
grounds that reach further.  Two later studies, of GPT-4o in 2025
\citep{linardon2025} and of three models in 2026 with web search switched off
\citep{seifi2026},
extend the period, so the claim can read ``LLMs tested from 2023 to
2026 invented references''.  It still does not cover models no one has
tested.  This is row S1-1 of Table~\ref{tab:examples}.

% S3-1: Toulmin's elements used by others (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Others have put Toulmin's elements to work (Section~\ref{sec:related}).  Micropublications build on his
layout to record the claims of (mainly biomedical) publications
\citep{clark2014}.  Kane's argument-based validation analyses the
inferences from test scores to their uses with his model
\citep[pp.~11--12]{kane2013}.  Two studies of university students' essays
scored argument quality element by element, with two raters each
\citep{yang2022,divya2026}.  None of these was built to check the reasoning of a
research paper.  The register applies them to each claim a paper makes,
as the rest of this section sets out.

\paragraph{Checks by kind of grounds.}
In the register (Figure~\ref{fig:register}), each claim is recorded with its grounds item by item,
and each kind of grounds gets its own check:
\begin{itemize}
% S3-9: Relayed points can gain unfounded authority (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
\item a \textbf{source} is checked in three steps: the work exists; it
  says what the claim attributes to it, for the cases and period the
  claim covers; and it reports the point as its own finding rather than
  relaying another work's.  A point repeated from paper to paper can
  gain authority no data gave it \citep{greenberg2009}, so a relayed
  point is traced to its origin or recorded as relayed.  The check also
  records whether the source is peer-reviewed, which
  Table~\ref{tab:confidence} counts.  That is read from where the source
  was published: the register trusts the source's peer review and does
  not redo it;
\item a \textbf{declared starting point}, an assumption, definition
  or value the paper adopts without arguing for it, is checked for two
  things: that the paper states it, and how the field regards it.  The
  record says whether it is the paper's own, contested, or accepted.  To
  record it as accepted, the author cites a source that shows the field
  accepts it; without one, the record says the field's view is not
  known;
\item the author's \textbf{own work}, such as a search or a trial, is
  checked for a method someone else could repeat;
\item a step from \textbf{other claims}, or from several sources to a
  point none of them makes alone, gets the warrant check.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The warrant check.}
The warrant check asks two questions, taken from the definition of a
cogent argument that Wachsmuth and colleagues adopt from Johnson and
Blair.  \emph{Relevance}: does each ground bear on this claim, giving a
reader some reason to accept it?  \emph{Sufficiency}: do the grounds together justify the
claim at the strength its qualifier states?  These are not the
logician's necessary and sufficient conditions.  Relevance asks whether
a ground counts for the claim at all; sufficiency asks whether all the
grounds together are enough.  A sufficiency check names
what is missing, such as a case not covered, a search not run or a
premise that rests on one study.  The check also records two further
things.  The claim's scope is the limit the author accepts: the cases
and conditions the claim covers, and those it does not.  An objection
is a reason to doubt the claim within that scope; the check records the
strongest known one and the answer to it.  Where a step follows a common pattern,
such as an argument from expert opinion or from the absence of search
results, the questions proper to that pattern could be added; they are not yet worked out (Section~\ref{sec:recs}).

The check has four outcomes: it passes, it passes and someone other than
the author confirms it, it leaves a named gap open, or it fails.  A
failed check means that the recorded grounds do not carry the claim as
worded, not that the claim is false; rewording the claim to what the
grounds carry is the usual response.

% S3-6: Calibrated language has precedents (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S3-7: Readers interpret fixed terms inconsistently (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% S3-3: Mapping tiers to wording (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D6: Tier-to-wording mapping is this paper's proposal (assumption, own)
\paragraph{Tiers and wording.}
Fixed wording for levels of certainty has precedents.  \citet{kent1964}
argued for ``a few key odds expressions'' in intelligence estimates.  The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) asks its authors to
judge confidence from the ``type, amount, quality, and consistency of
evidence'' and ``the degree of agreement'' \citep{mastrandrea2010}.  The GRADE working group (Grading of Recommendations Assessment,
Development and Evaluation) fixes wording per level of certainty and size
of effect, from ``results in'' to ``may result in'' \citep{santesso2020}.
Fixed terms may not secure a shared reading: when readers assigned
numbers to the probability terms in an IPCC report, their judgements
deviated from the IPCC's guidelines even when the guidelines were at
hand \citep{budescu2009}.

Table~\ref{tab:confidence} adapts the
practice for academic papers.  A claim's tier is the lower of two
ratings: how well its grounds are supported, and what its warrant check
allows.  An argued claim can therefore reach the Supported tier when its
premises are supported and its check passes, which a rule that only
counts sources does not allow.  The table may help an author notice
when a sentence claims more than its grounds allow.

A register that passes its checks means that the paper followed the
method: each claim's grounds and warrant were recorded and checked
against stated criteria.  It does not mean that the claims are true.  A
check run by the author can err in the author's favour, so a claim
reaches the Established tier only when someone else confirms its check.
Peer review would be the usual place for that confirmation.

\centering
\caption{Confidence tiers and the qualifiers they permit (proposed).  A
claim takes the lower of the two ratings.}
\label{tab:confidence}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2cm}p{4.9cm}p{4.2cm}p{3.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Support for the grounds} & \textbf{Warrant check} & \textbf{Qualifiers} \\
\midrule
Established & Three or more independent sources agree, at least two of them peer-reviewed; no known dispute & Passed and confirmed by someone other than the author, normally in peer review & ``shows'', ``confirms'' \\

\smallskip\raggedright A claim about what one source says (``Kane
defines validation as\ldots'') rests on that source alone: it reaches Supported when its check
passes, and Established when someone else confirms the check.  A claim
that rests on other claims takes a tier no higher than the weakest of
them.  Searches and design trials are rated by method.  Sources that relay the same origin count as one.
\end{table}

\paragraph{Priority.}
Every claim also carries a \emph{priority}, the author's answer to the
question of what fails if the claim is wrong (Box~1).  Priority sets how much checking a claim deserves, and every P0 entry,
the contribution included, should reach the Supported tier.  A paper whose central claims stay at
Emerging falls short of that floor.

% S3-2: Whetten as a check of the contribution (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
\paragraph{The contribution.}
The contribution's entry is checked with Whetten's account.  He set out what ``a
complete theory must contain'': which factors (\emph{What}), how they
relate (\emph{How}), why (\emph{Why}), and for whom, where and when it
holds (\emph{Who, where, when}) \citep[pp.~490--492]{whetten1989}.
His seven reviewer questions, from ``What's new?'' to ``Who cares?'',
answer ``What constitutes a publishable theory paper?''
\citep[p.~494]{whetten1989}, so they may serve as the check of a paper's contribution.  This
paper's own contribution is a recommendation, and there they fit less
well.  \emph{How} then tends to restate the recommendation itself, so
the check also needs the value behind it and the conditions under which
it applies (Table~\ref{tab:whetten}).

% D8: Verification and validation mapping (assumption, own)
These two levels map onto a distinction used across engineering.
Verification is ``proof of compliance with requirements''; validation
shows that a product ``accomplishes the intended purpose in the intended
environment'' \citep[\S2.0]{nasa2016}.  Checking each claim against its
grounds and warrant is verification in this sense; asking Whetten's
questions of the contribution is closer to validation.  The register then works as a traceability record, like those engineers keep for requirements.  Each link is
written down, from the contribution to its claims, from a claim to its
grounds and from grounds to their sources, so that someone else can
follow and check it.  The IPCC asks its authors for a ``traceable account'' of
their evaluation of evidence for the same reason \citep{mastrandrea2010}.

% S3-4: Citation checks and source counts can misjudge the step (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
\paragraph{Why check by kind of grounds.}
A citation check does not look at the step from grounds to claim, so it
can pass a claim that says more than its sources carry; a rating that
counts sources can also fail a sound inference with no source to count.
The worked example above is a false pass of this kind.
% S3-12: Checks chosen by the kind of grounds may avoid both errors (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
A check chosen by the kind of grounds (a source against the claim's scope,
an inference against its qualifier) looks at that step and may avoid both
errors.

% S1-4: Recent AI-writing checkers check evidence (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Recent tools for AI-assisted writing go beyond checking that references
exist.  One automated checker also checks causal language against the study design
\citep{samsonau2026}.  One workflow returns unsupported claims to the
author \citep{chen2026}, and a provenance discipline samples whether the
evidence suffices \citep{zhou2026}.  A checklist for cited assertions in
narrative reviews is in development \citep{vanra2026}.  As their authors describe them, each asks whether
evidence exists and supports a claim; Zhou and Yu judge sufficiency only
on a sample.  None describes a check, for
each claim in the prose, of whether its grounds bear on it and justify
its wording, and what would make the step fail.

The appraisal tools come closer to the reasoning.  The evidence-synthesis
organisation JBI has a consensus-based checklist for appraising expert
opinion; it asks ``Does the opinion
demonstrate a logically defended argument to support the conclusions
drawn?'' and refers reviewers to Toulmin's model
\citep{mcarthur2025,jbi2026}.  SANRA, a scale for narrative reviews,
scores scientific reasoning as one of its six items, and its authors
call it ``not a reporting guideline'' \citep{baethge2019}.  Both judge an argument as a whole.

% S4-6: Assurance cases link claims, arguments and evidence (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Two practices record the structure of an argument explicitly.  In safety engineering, an assurance case, or safety case, is ``a
documented body of evidence that provides a convincing and valid
argument that a system is adequately safe'' for its use
\citep{bishop1998}.  Its authors ``make an explicit
set of claims'', link them to the evidence through arguments, and ``make
clear the assumptions and judgements underlying the arguments''
\citep{bishop1998}.  The Goal Structuring Notation breaks claims into
sub-claims until each can be supported by evidence \citep{kelly2004},
and the claims--arguments--evidence approach defines its arguments as
``Toulmin's warrants'' and treats claims asserted without justification
as assumptions \citep{bloomfield2014}.

% S4-7: Micropublications and nanopublications record claims (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
In scholarly data publishing, nanopublications express a core
scientific statement with its context and provenance \citep{groth2010}.  Micropublications record a statement with its
attribution and, at their fullest, with the evidence and challenges
brought for and against it; their model ``is grounded in
Toulmin-Verheij'' \citep{clark2014}.  Both make claims and their support
machine-readable.  Neither describes a procedure for checking a step.

% S4-8: Argument-based validation (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Educational measurement has an example of checking an
argument step by step.  In Kane's argument-based approach, the claims
made from test scores are laid out as an argument that specifies the
inferences and assumptions they need.  Validation then evaluates the coherence and completeness of that argument and the plausibility of
its inferences and assumptions \citep{kane2013}.  The object is the use
of a test, not a paper.

% S4-5: Argument-quality research supplies criteria (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Research on argument quality supplies criteria for the step itself.
\citet{wachsmuth2017} distinguish logical, rhetorical and dialectical
quality, and adopt Johnson and Blair's definition of a cogent argument as one with ``individually
acceptable premises that are relevant to the argument's conclusion and,
together, sufficient to draw the conclusion''.  \citet{walton2008}
collect argumentation schemes, which their publisher describes as
``forms of argument that capture stereotypical patterns of human
reasoning''.

% S4-9: The register combines what the approaches hold apart (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Each of the four parts therefore already has users, one of them only in part
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}), but none of the approaches compared here
combines them for an academic paper.  The claim register is built to combine them.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The register has one user so far: the author of this paper.  Whether
it works in other hands, and whether it helps anyone find weak steps,
has not been tested.  Its
reviews came from language models of five vendors, in sessions
separate from the drafting.  They are independent of it but not of the method, and no substitute
for human reviewers.

The register asks work of authors; this paper's has 41 entries.
A shorter form, the warrant check's questions for a reviewer to ask of
the central claims, may suit many papers better.  Toulmin's and
Whetten's frameworks come from Western academic traditions, and
scholars in the humanities may find a claim-by-claim layout foreign to
how their arguments work; their fit with other conventions needs to be
assessed.

The checks are also partly circular: the author who wrote the rules
applies them, and the warrant check is a judgement, recorded with its
reasons so that someone else can disagree with it.
% S3-11: The register inherits the reliability of the peer review it trusts (descriptive, P2, EMERGING)
The register also inherits the reliability of the peer review it
trusts: a source that passed review can still be wrong, and the
register does not check the reasoning inside its sources.

% D10: The author is responsible for every entry (value, own)
Who fills in a register matters.  Authors are responsible for every
entry.  An AI tool may draft
entries, but a
source check needs the source in hand: a tool that has not read the
source produces only another claim to be checked.
% S3-10: Who writes the warrant may matter (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Whether a register
makes the author's reasoning explicit or replaces part of it may depend
on who writes the warrant.  Writing a warrant asks the author to state
the step, and a missing step shows as an empty cell; checking a warrant
a tool wrote may ask less, and a filled cell looks like finished
reasoning.  People relying on imperfect decision aids miss what the aid
fails to flag, even when told to verify its advice
\citep[pp.~391, 397]{parasuraman2010}, and knowledge workers more
confident in generative AI report less critical thinking, shifted
towards verifying the tool's output \citep[pp.~1, 9]{lee2025chi}.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recs}

% C1: A published register can make reasoning traceable; test it (contribution, P0, EMERGING)
% D1: Published reasoning should be easily checkable (value, own)
% D2: Verification infrastructure (definition, own)
% D7: A proposal should be tested before it is adopted (value, own)
The value behind this proposal is that published reasoning should be
easily checkable: readers and reviewers should be able to see what each claim
rests on.  A claim register, published with a non-empirical paper, could make that reasoning visible and traceable.  I propose
two steps, in this order.  Authors and journals could publish registers
as optional supplementary material, as this paper does, and trials
should test three things: whether authors who keep one find weak steps
of their own, whether reviewers who have one find different weaknesses
from reviewers who do not, and whether warrants an author writes differ
from warrants a tool drafts.  A register should become part of
verification infrastructure for non-empirical papers, of the kind
EQUATOR offers for empirical health research, only if such trials find
that it helps.  A first implementation exists: templates for the
register and the glossary, the checks for each kind of grounds, and
tools that test a register against its manuscript (see Code and Data
Availability).

The register could be used beyond scholarly articles: for the interpretive parts of empirical papers, for journalism, or in teaching, where its checks might help students learn to argue.  Peer review already judges arguments, and a
register would not replace it.  It could show reviewers where a paper's
weight rests, and it could help authors find weak steps before
submission; whether it does either is the question the trials should
answer.

% S5-2: Next steps (normative, P2, SPECULATIVE)
Several further directions warrant investigation.  The gap search needs
a second screener and wider databases.  The questions proper to common
patterns of argument need working out, and the checks need adapting to
other fields.
