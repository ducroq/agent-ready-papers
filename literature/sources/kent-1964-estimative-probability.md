- **Status:** READ (full text, 2026-10-16). The reprint carries no printed page numbers; locations below are PDF pages of the 21-page reprint.

## Summary
Argues "for consistent, unambiguous usage of a few key odds expressions" in intelligence estimates. Kent separates statements of fact from estimates of knowable-but-unknown and unknowable things, recounts how the phrase "serious possibility" in NIE 29-51 was read at very different odds by its own authors and its readers, and proposes a five-step table that ties verbal expressions to numerical probability bands. He closes by weighing the "poets" (who resist fixed odds) against the "mathematicians".

## Key Findings
- Subtitle (PDF p. 1): "The case for consistent, unambiguous usage of a few key odds expressions".
- A qualifier signals evidential strength: "some sort of verbal qualifier is necessary to show that the case is a 90-percenter, not a 100" (PDF p. 2).
- The failure case: after agreeing the wording "serious possibility", "each Board member had had somewhat different odds in mind" (PDF p. 4).
- An 11-step first draft was abandoned because "the verbal equivalent could not possibly convey that exactness" (PDF p. 7).
- The final chart (PDF p. 8) has five bands: almost certain (93%, "Give or take almost 6%"), probable (75%, "Give or take about 12"%), chances about even (50%, ±10%), probably not (30%, ±10%), almost certainly not (7%, ±5%).
- Calibration is addressed to readers: when an estimate says unlikely, "we mean that the chances of its NOT happening are in our judgment about three to one" (PDF p. 8).

## Relevance to agent-ready-papers
The earliest precedent we hold for tying verbal confidence terms to a fixed scale, older than IPCC (L96) by four decades. Paper 1 draft 2 could cite it as the origin of the calibrated-language idea behind DR-002's tier-to-language mapping, and its NIE 29-51 anecdote suggests why a fixed vocabulary matters: the same phrase was read at different odds by writers and readers. One difference may need stating: Kent maps words to probabilities of events, whereas DR-002 maps words to evidence tiers, so the analogy is partial.
