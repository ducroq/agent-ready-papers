## Summary
Defines the safety case and argues that its argument, not its evidence, is the part most often neglected. Presents GSN, a graphical notation whose goal structures decompose goals (claims) through strategies into sub-goals until they rest on solutions (evidence), with context elements stating scope. It surveys industrial use (Eurofighter, Jubilee Line and others) and extensions: maintenance, patterns and anti-patterns, modular cases, and a qualitative "safety assurance" scheme for confidence in an argument. The text does not mention Toulmin.

## Key Findings
- Definition (PDF p. 1): "A safety case should communicate a clear, comprehensive and defensible argument that a system is acceptably safe to operate in a particular context".
- Elements (PDF p. 1): "The safety case consists of three principal elements: Requirements, Argument and Evidence".
- The neglected argument (PDF p. 2): "The reader is often left to guess at an unwritten and implicit argument"; "Argument without supporting evidence is unfounded, and therefore unconvincing. Evidence without argument is unexplained".
- GSN "explicitly represents the individual elements of any safety argument (requirements, claims, evidence and context)" (PDF p. 2), showing "how goals (claims about the system) are successively broken down into sub-goals until a point is reached where claims can be supported by direct reference to available evidence (solutions)" (PDF p. 3).
- Confidence (PDF p. 5): "While GSN describes the relationship between premises and conclusions, it does not capture the inductive nature of the safety argument". Safety assurance is defined as "A qualitative statement expressing the degree of confidence that a safety claim is true" (PDF p. 5); "By expressing the assurance, these judgements are made explicit within the argument, allowing other readers to agree or disagree" (PDF p. 6).

## Relevance to agent-ready-papers
GSN may be the nearest engineering precedent for a claim registry: both make the argument's claims explicit and tie each one to evidence, and Kelly & Weaver's "unwritten and implicit argument" reads much like the problem draft 2 attributes to AI-assisted prose. Their qualitative assurance levels also resemble DR-002's tiers. Paper 1 draft 2 could position its proposal as adapting this idea to academic writing rather than inventing it. The differences that may need stating are that a safety case argues that one system is acceptably safe for a regulator, whereas a paper's registry tracks many loosely coupled claims and ties each confidence level to the wording of the prose. Whether that difference amounts to novelty is a judgement for the paper, not something this source settles.
