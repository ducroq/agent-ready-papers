% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Generative AI tools now help write a growing share of academic papers.
Checking whether their citations exist has received attention, but a
paper whose contribution is an argument can cite only real sources and
still reason badly.  Reporting guidelines appear to offer little help
here.  The
EQUATOR (Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research)
Network lists about 700 guidelines for empirical health research, and
I found no consensus checklist for verifying the reasoning of
non-empirical papers.  This commentary argues that the vocabulary for
such a checklist already exists.  Toulmin's layout of arguments
treats every assertion as a claim that rests on grounds through a
warrant and carries a qualifier; Whetten's criteria judge a theoretical contribution as a
whole.  Combined, they allow a procedure that I propose: register each claim
with what it rests on, check each item by its kind, and check the
contribution once.  The paper applies this procedure to itself and
publishes the record.  It reports no evaluation; whether the procedure
helps authors and reviewers is an open question.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} academic writing, generative AI, verification,
argumentation, reporting guidelines, non-empirical research
\end{abstract}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Key Points:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Generative AI tools can produce fluent papers whose citations are real and whose reasoning is still weak.
\item Reporting guidelines cover empirical research; I found no consensus checklist for verifying the reasoning of non-empirical papers.
\item Toulmin and Whetten supply a vocabulary for such a checklist: each claim is checked by what it rests on, and the contribution is checked once.
\item The paper publishes its own claim registry as a worked example; it does not show that the procedure works.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}

Writing assistants based on large language models (LLMs) have spread
quickly: up to 22\% of computer science papers now show signs of LLM
editing, and such editing can be detected in every major discipline
% D4: AI-augmented writing (definition, accepted)
\citep{liang2025}.  In this paper ``generative AI tools'', or ``AI'' for
short, means these assistants.  They write fluently, and they can
synthesise literature, structure an argument, draft prose and format
references.  When a human author falls short, the text tends to show it,
in awkward phrasing, gaps in coverage or inconsistent formatting.  An
AI-assisted draft can read as polished and complete while it contains
errors that only checking would reveal, such as a citation to a paper
that does not exist.

% S1-7: Fluent, well-cited prose can carry weak reasoning (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% S1-1: Citation hallucination (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
Citation hallucination is the most discussed of these errors.
\citet{mugaanyi2024} found that 62--89\% of the digital object
identifiers (DOIs) that LLMs generated for natural-science and humanities
topics were fabricated, and \citet{walters2023} found fabricated
citations in 55\% of references generated by OpenAI's GPT-3.5 model and
18\% of those by GPT-4 in
generated literature reviews.  A fabricated citation cannot be caught by
knowing the field, because the work it cites does not exist; it can be
caught by looking the work up.

% S1-2: Confidence inflation (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
A second error is harder to see.  I call it \emph{confidence
inflation}: wording that expresses more certainty than the evidence
supports.  The evidence indicates that LLMs verbalise high confidence
even on questions they answer wrongly \citep{xiong2024,zhou2024relying}
and that their summaries of scientific work generalise findings beyond
what the original studies claim \citep{peters2025}.  Whether this
carries over into academic prose is unsettled: in one corpus of
applied-linguistics abstracts, ChatGPT used boosters such as ``shows''
at twice the human rate, but also more hedges \citep{eldakhs2025}.  The
stakes are epistemic, because words such as ``demonstrates'' and
``suggests'' are meant to tell a reader how much weight a claim can bear.

% S1-5: Calculation errors (descriptive, P1, SPECULATIVE)
In technical papers a third error warrants investigation: derived
numbers that look plausible and are wrong.  In informal trials on my own projects,
prompting a model to recompute every value caught arithmetic errors that
prompting it to judge soundness missed; this is an observation, not a
measured result.

% S1-4: Existing tools check evidence, not reasoning (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Recent systems go beyond checking that references exist.  A linter
checks a manuscript's references, internal consistency and causal
language against the study design \citep{samsonau2026}; a workflow pairs
each drafted claim with an evidence packet and returns unsupported
claims to the author \citep{chen2026}; a provenance discipline binds
reported quantities to an audit trail \citep{zhou2026}; and a checklist
for verifying cited assertions in narrative reviews is in development
\citep{vanra2026}.  Each asks whether evidence exists, supports a claim
or suffices, and the linter also checks causal wording against the study
design.  None that I found asks whether a warrant holds for its readers,
or chooses the check by what a claim rests on, which is where a paper
whose contribution is an argument can fail.

% S1-6: Detection is no substitute (descriptive, P2, EMERGING)
% D5: Detection vs verification (definition, own)
Detecting AI-written text does not fill that gap.  Detection asks who
wrote a text; verification asks whether it holds.  Detectors have also
proved fragile in tests: their accuracy varies with generation settings
\citep{dugan2024}, and paraphrase can collapse it \citep{krishna2023}.

\section{Reporting Guidelines and Non-Empirical Papers}

% S2-1: EQUATOR (descriptive, P0, SUPPORTED)
A well-developed example of verification infrastructure for academic
writing is the EQUATOR Network, which lists about 700 reporting
guidelines for health research \citepalias{equator}.  Guidelines such as CONSORT for
randomised trials and PRISMA for systematic reviews \citep{page2021} give
authors a checklist to complete while they prepare a manuscript, and the
GoodReports tool picks the checklist that fits a study type
\citep{struthers2021}.  For one guideline the effect has been measured: journals endorsing CONSORT reported more completely on 25 of
27 checklist items, significantly so for five \citep{turner2012}, and
incomplete reporting is a recognised source of research waste
\citep{glasziou2014}.

% S2-2: No consensus checklist for argument-level verification (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
Guidance for papers without new data exists, but it is scattered and
seems not to reach the reasoning.  Conceptual papers lack ``commonly
accepted templates'' \citep[p.~18]{jaakkola2020}, although Jaakkola
proposes four.  Design-science researchers have reported no agreed
guidance on presenting their work \citep{gregor2013,dinter2015}, and
validity in that field ``is neither well-understood nor consistently
established'' \citep{larsen2025}.  Software engineering's community
standards ask methodological papers for ``clear, valid arguments'' but
give no procedure for checking them \citep{ralph2020}.  A quality scale
for narrative reviews exists, though its authors call it ``not a
reporting guideline'' \citep{baethge2019}, and reporting items for
concept analysis have only recently been collected \citep{nashwan2026}.
I found no consensus checklist for verifying the reasoning of such
papers: whether each step from evidence to claim holds.

% S2-3a: Gregor's five theory types (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S2-3b: Types I and V are non-empirical (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D3: Non-empirical paper (definition, own)
\citet{gregor2006} distinguishes five types of theory in information
systems research.  Two of them, analytic theories (Type~I) and theories
for design and action (Type~V), contribute conceptual structures more
than findings, and such contributions may not be checkable against an
earlier source, because they are the new contribution.  Theoretical,
conceptual, design-science and perspective papers are examples of
non-empirical papers in this sense; the list is not a classification.

% S2-4: Argument quality as the main challenge (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Building on \citet{toulmin2003} and \citet{whetten1989}, I suggest that
for these papers the main question may be less ``do the data support the
claim?'' and more ``does the reasoning hold?''.  That would be a
different task from the one reporting guidelines support.

\section{Toulmin and Whetten as a Basis for Verification}

Two well-known frameworks may offer much of what a checklist for
reasoning needs.  Box~1 gives the terms; the framework's glossary defines them
from their sources.\footnote{The glossary, the claim registry and the
checking tools are public; see Code and Data Availability.}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption*{\textbf{Box 1.} Terms used in this paper.}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}p{11.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
Claim & Any assertion put forward for acceptance. \\
Grounds & What a claim rests on (Toulmin's \emph{data}): a source, other claims, a declared starting point, or the author's own observation. \\
Warrant & The step from grounds to claim. \\
Qualifier & The hedge that states how strong the claim is. \\
Confidence tier & The rating (Established, Supported, Emerging, Speculative) that decides which qualifiers a claim may carry. \\
Priority & How much the paper depends on a claim (P0 to P2). \\
Rebuttal & Circumstances in which the step from grounds to claim no longer holds. \\
Declared starting point & An assumption, definition or value the paper adopts without arguing for it. \\
Contribution & What the paper adds; checked once, as a whole. \\
Verification & Checking each claim against its grounds and warrant. \\
Validation & Checking that the contribution serves its readers and purpose. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

% S3-5: Toulmin's layout and its origin (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
Toulmin modelled his layout of arguments on jurisprudence.  He called
logic ``generalised jurisprudence'' and likened the
difference between data and warrant to ``the distinction drawn in the
law-courts between questions of fact and questions of law''
\citep[pp.~7, 92]{toulmin2003}.  In his account any assertion is a
claim; its \emph{data} are ``the facts we appeal to as a foundation for
the claim''; its warrant is one of the ``general, hypothetical
statements, which can act as bridges'' from data to claim; a
\emph{qualifier} indicates ``the strength conferred by the warrant on
this step''; and a \emph{rebuttal} names ``circumstances in which the
general authority of the warrant would have to be set aside''
\citep[pp.~90--94]{toulmin2003}.  Toulmin later wrote that he had not had in mind an analytical model
like the one that came to be called ``the Toulmin model''; his concern
``was with twentieth-century epistemology, not informal logic''
\citep[p.~vii]{toulmin2003}.  Using his layout as a
checklist is my adaptation, and it has to be judged on its own merits.

% S3-1: Toulmin's elements as criteria for assessing argument quality (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
That adaptation looks workable.  Two studies of university students'
essays scored argument quality element by element on Toulmin's layout,
with two raters each \citep{yang2022,divya2026}, which indicates that
the elements can serve as assessment criteria; the second study adapted
the first one's rubric, and both concern student essays, so applying the
elements to verify research papers is a further step.  LLMs can also
extract Toulmin's elements from text when prompted with them
\citep{gupta2024}, so a tool may help an author lay out what each claim
rests on.

The procedure I propose treats a paper's claims as differing less in
kind than in what they rest on.  The grounds of a claim may be a cited source, other claims in the
paper, a declared starting point such as a definition or a value, or
the author's own observation.  In the procedure, each kind of grounds
gets its own check:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{source} is checked in two steps: the work exists, and it
  says what the claim attributes to it;
\item a step from \textbf{other claims} is checked by asking whether the
  warrant holds for the paper's readers;
\item a \textbf{declared starting point} is checked only for being stated,
  with a note on whether the field accepts or contests it;
\item an \textbf{own observation} is checked for a method someone else
  could repeat.
\end{itemize}
Combining several sources into a point none of them makes alone adds an
inference, which needs the warrant check.  A recommendation needs two
things more: a declared value it serves, and the conditions under which
it applies.

% S3-2: Whetten can be operationalised (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Whetten's criteria work at a different level.  He set out what ``a
complete theory must contain'': which factors (What), how they relate
(How), why (``the theoretical glue that welds the model together''), and
for whom, where and when it holds \citep[pp.~490--492]{whetten1989}.
His seven reviewer questions, from ``What's new?'' to ``Who cares?'',
answer ``What constitutes a publishable theory paper?''
\citep[p.~494]{whetten1989}.  They judge a contribution as a whole, so
they may serve best as a check of the paper's central contribution,
applied once, and not of each statement in it.

% D8: Verification and validation mapping (assumption, own)
These two levels map onto a distinction from systems engineering.
Verification shows ``proof of compliance with requirements''; validation
shows that a product ``accomplishes the intended purpose in the intended
environment'' \citep[\S2.0]{nasa2016}.  Checking each claim against its grounds
and warrant is verification in this sense; asking Whetten's questions of
the contribution is closer to validation.  The mapping is mine.

Verification in this sense needs requirements to check against, and the
procedure supplies them.  A claim meets its requirements when every item
it rests on has passed the check for its kind, its warrant holds for the
paper's readers, and its qualifier is one that its confidence tier
permits.  The contribution's requirements are Whetten's elements.  The
registry then works as a traceability record, of the kind systems
engineers keep for requirements: each link, from the contribution to its
claims, from a claim to its grounds and from grounds to their sources, is
written down, so that someone else can follow and check it.  The IPCC
asks its authors for a ``traceable account'' of their evaluation of
evidence for the same reason \citep{mastrandrea2010}.

% S3-6: Calibrated language has precedents (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
% S3-3: Mapping tiers to wording (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
% D6: Tier-to-wording mapping is this paper's proposal (assumption, own)
Qualifiers need a scale.  Fixed wording for levels of certainty has
precedents.  \citet{kent1964} argued for ``a few key odds expressions''
in intelligence estimates; the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change
(IPCC) asks its authors
to judge confidence from the ``type, amount, quality, and consistency of
evidence'' and ``the degree of agreement'' \citep{mastrandrea2010}; and the GRADE
working group (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and
Evaluation) fixes wording per level of certainty and size of effect, from
``results in'' to ``may result in'' \citep{santesso2020}.  Table~1
adapts this practice with four confidence tiers, each permitting certain
qualifiers.  It assigns tiers mainly by counting agreeing sources, which
is simpler than the IPCC's two axes and has not been tested with
readers, as GRADE's wording was.  It may still help an author notice
when a sentence claims more than its grounds allow.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Confidence tiers and the qualifiers they permit.}
\label{tab:confidence}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Assigned when\ldots} & \textbf{Qualifiers} \\
\midrule
Established & Multiple independent sources; no dispute & ``shows'', ``confirms'' \\
Supported   & Two or three sources agree; open questions remain & ``indicates'', ``evidence suggests'' \\
Emerging    & One or two sources; not replicated & ``may'', ``preliminary evidence'' \\
Speculative & Inference only; no direct evidence & ``warrants investigation'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every claim also carries a \emph{priority}, the author's answer to the
question of what fails if the claim is wrong.  A P0 claim is one the
paper fails without; P1 strengthens the argument; P2 gives context.  The
priority sets how complete the checking must be, and every P0 claim is
expected to reach the Supported tier.

% S3-4: Each claim needs the checks its grounds call for (descriptive, P0, EMERGING)
The central argument of this paper builds on these pieces.  If a
claim is checked by a procedure that does not fit what it rests on, the
check may fail it for the wrong reason: an inference judged by whether
it has a source looks unsupported even when its reasoning holds.
Recording each claim's grounds item by item, and letting the kind of
grounds decide the check, may avoid such false failures.  The argument
rests on design reasoning and on this paper's own registry; it has not
been tested across authors or fields.

\section{Limitations}

% S4-6: Assurance cases are the closest existing practice (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
The closest existing practice comes from safety engineering.  An
assurance case, or safety case, is ``a documented body of evidence that
provides a convincing and valid argument that a system is adequately
safe'' for its use; its authors ``make an explicit set of claims'',
link them to the evidence through arguments, and ``make clear the
assumptions and judgements underlying the arguments''
\citep{bishop1998}.  The Goal Structuring Notation breaks claims into
sub-claims until each can be supported by evidence \citep{kelly2004},
and the claims--arguments--evidence approach defines its arguments as
``Toulmin's warrants'' and treats claims asserted without justification
as assumptions \citep{bloomfield2014}, much as declared starting points
work here.  The procedure proposed in this paper borrows that
discipline for academic writing.  It assures the reasoning of a paper
where an assurance case assures the safety of a system, and it adds a
check of the contribution as a whole.

% S4-5: Argument-quality research offers more structure (descriptive, P1, SUPPORTED)
The vocabulary leaves what is probably the hardest part, judging whether
a warrant holds, to the reader.  Research on argument quality offers more structure.
\citet{wachsmuth2017} distinguish logical, rhetorical and dialectical
quality and define a cogent argument as one with ``individually
acceptable premises that are relevant to the argument's conclusion and,
together, sufficient to draw the conclusion''; \citet{walton2008}
collect argumentation schemes, which their publisher describes as
``forms of argument that capture stereotypical patterns of human
reasoning''.  Either could make the
warrant check more specific than Toulmin's layout does.

% S4-3: Structure plus LLMs (descriptive, P1, EMERGING)
Whether tools can carry part of the load is also open.  Structured
prompting was reported to improve LLM argument extraction
\citep{gupta2024}, and representing support and attack formally to
improve LLM review aggregation \citep{peerarg2024}.  Both are narrow tasks, so they suggest
only that structure combined with LLMs may outperform LLMs alone.

The tier rule is a simplification.  Counting sources does not measure
their independence or quality, and it gives no guidance for an inference,
whose strength lies in its warrant.  And the procedure has one user so
far: the author of this paper.  Verification by someone other than the
author would carry more weight.  The closest step so far was a review
of the first draft by four language models from different vendors, which
is independent of the drafting session but not of the method, and no
substitute for human reviewers.  This commentary is a first step that
proposes a way of checking.  It does not show that the way works, which
needs authors and reviewers in several fields to use it and a comparison
with papers checked without it.

\section{Recommendations}

% C1: Verification infrastructure for non-empirical papers (contribution, P0, EMERGING)
% D1: Published reasoning should be checkable (value, accepted)
% D2: Verification infrastructure (definition, own)
I propose that the scholarly community develop verification
infrastructure for non-empirical papers, as EQUATOR did for empirical
health research.  The value behind the proposal is that published
reasoning should be checkable: readers and reviewers should be able to
see what each claim rests on.  Its grounds are the gap described in Section~2 and the argument of
Section~3.  A first version could be modest: authors register
the claims a paper depends on, with their grounds, warrants and
qualifiers, and publish the register with the paper, as this one does.  A first
implementation exists: templates for the registry and the glossary,
checks for each kind of grounds, and tools that test a registry against
its manuscript (see Code and Data Availability).

The proposal applies to academic papers whose contribution is an
argument, and to the interpretive parts of empirical papers.  The same
procedure might serve other writing that asks readers to trust its
reasoning, journalism among them, but that is untested.  Toulmin's and
Whetten's frameworks come from Western academic traditions; their fit
with other scholarly conventions needs to be assessed.

Peer review already judges arguments, and a register would not replace
it.  It could help authors find weak steps before submission and help
reviewers see where a paper's weight rests.

% S5-2: Next steps (normative, P2, SPECULATIVE)
% D7: A proposal should be tested before it is adopted (value, accepted)
Several directions warrant investigation: testing whether registers
change what reviewers find, adapting the checks to the conventions of
other fields, building them into writing tools so that the checks run
while an author drafts, and recognising the recomputation of derived
values as a check of its own.

\paragraph{What the registry shows about this paper.}
Of the six P0 entries, three reach the Supported tier or above and three
do not: the gap claim (S2-2), the central argument (S3-4) and the
contribution (C1), which rests on both.  By the framework's own rule the
paper does not pass its gate for critical claims.  That is reported here
because a framework that asks authors to state the strength of their
evidence should state its own.
