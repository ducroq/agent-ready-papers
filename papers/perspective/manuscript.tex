% ABSTRACT
% ===============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Large language models are increasingly used to assist with academic
writing.  Citation hallucination has received considerable attention,
but AI writing assistants also create verification challenges that
existing tools do not address: confidence inflation, scope creep, and
fluent prose that hides gaps in reasoning.  A gap in reporting
guidelines compounds the problem.  The EQUATOR Network maintains nearly 700 guidelines for
empirical health research, yet to our knowledge no consensus-based
equivalent exists for non-empirical paper types.
Drawing on Gregor's (2006) theory-type taxonomy, we identify two
non-empirical theory types for which argument quality, more than factual
accuracy, is the primary verification challenge.  We propose a typed
verification model that distinguishes claims (source-verifiable facts),
arguments (reasoning chains checked with Toulmin's model), and
propositions (recommendations with boundary conditions, checked with
Whetten's criteria), each with its own procedure.  We propose that the
scholarly community develop verification infrastructure for
non-empirical papers, analogous to what EQUATOR provides for empirical
research.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} academic writing, artificial intelligence, reporting
guidelines, verification, argumentation, non-empirical research
\end{abstract}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Key Points:}
\begin{itemize}
\item AI writing tools create verification challenges beyond citation hallucination, including confidence inflation and argument degradation.
\item EQUATOR provides nearly 700 guidelines for empirical research; we found no consensus-based equivalent for non-empirical papers.
\item We propose typed verification that distinguishes claims, arguments, and propositions, each with its own procedure.
\item Applying the wrong verification procedure to a unit type may produce false failures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction: The Problem}

Writing assistants based on large language models (LLMs) have spread
quickly: up to 22\% of computer science papers now show signs of LLM
editing, and such editing can be detected in every major discipline
\citep{liang2025}.  The tools write fluently, and they can synthesise
literature, structure an argument, draft prose, and format references.
That fluency brings risks of its own, and some are harder to spot than
the problems of writing without AI.  When a human author falls short,
the text tends to show it, in awkward phrasing, gaps in coverage, or
inconsistent formatting.  An AI-assisted draft can read as polished and
complete while it contains errors that only checking would reveal, such
as a citation to a paper that does not exist.

% S1-1: Citation hallucination (CLAIM, P0, SUPPORTED)
The most widely discussed risk is citation hallucination.  LLMs can
invent plausible-sounding papers, complete with fabricated authors,
journal names, and digital object identifiers (DOIs).
\citet{mugaanyi2024} found that 62--89\% of DOIs generated by large
language models for natural-science and humanities topics were
fabricated, with DOI accuracy as low as 8.5\% in some disciplines.
\citet{walters2023} found fabricated citations in 55\% of GPT-3.5
references and 18\% of GPT-4 references in generated literature
reviews, lower for the newer model but still substantial.  A
hallucinated citation is a distinctive kind of falsehood: unlike a
factual error that contradicts known evidence, it cannot be caught
through domain knowledge alone, because the work it cites does not
exist.  Citation hallucination has received considerable attention, and
tools designed to detect it are emerging.

% S1-2: Confidence inflation (CLAIM, P0, SUPPORTED)
Citation hallucination may, however, be only the most visible failure
mode.  A second risk, which may be harder to notice, is what we term
\emph{confidence inflation}: AI writing assistants may express more
certainty than the evidence warrants.  LLMs verbalise high confidence
even on questions they answer wrongly \citep{xiong2024,zhou2024relying},
and LLM summaries of scientific work generalise findings beyond what the
original studies claim \citep{peters2025}.  Whether this reaches
academic prose is less clear.  In one corpus of applied-linguistics
abstracts, ChatGPT used boosters such as ``shows'' at twice the human
rate, but also more hedges \citep{eldakhs2025}, and no study we know of
yet measures boosters against the strength of the evidence.  The stakes
are epistemic.  ``Demonstrates'' and ``suggests'' tell readers whether a claim rests on strong evidence or on preliminary
findings, so inflated prose can misrepresent the state of the evidence
without containing a single factually incorrect statement.

% S1-5: Calculation errors (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
A third risk applies specifically to technical and empirical papers: AI-generated
equations and derived values can contain arithmetic errors that survive
review because the results look plausible.  A number in a physically
reasonable range can pass a plausibility review even when the arithmetic
behind it is wrong.  This suggests that for quantitative content,
\emph{mechanical reproduction} (substituting values and recomputing) catches errors that \emph{expert assessment} (judging whether the
physics looks right) does not.

% S1-3: Scope creep (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
A fourth risk is scope creep.  Without structural constraints such as
section specifications and word budgets, AI-assisted drafts may stretch
arguments beyond what the available evidence supports, add tangential
sections, and exceed format requirements.  Scope is hard to manage in
all academic writing, but AI tools can make it harder: they generate content with little effort, so expansion becomes the path of
least resistance.

% S1-4: No per-type verification procedure in existing infrastructure (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
None of these failure modes can be detected by checking whether
citations are real.  Tools that check automatically whether references
exist, and model-level approaches such as retrieval-augmented generation
(in which the model is given access to source documents while it
writes), address the factual accuracy of AI-assisted writing.  Some
recent systems go further.  A linter checks a manuscript's references,
internal consistency, and causal language against the study design
\citep{samsonau2026}; a workflow pairs each drafted claim with an
evidence packet and sends unsupported claims back to the author
\citep{chen2026}; a provenance discipline binds every
reported quantity to a sealed trail \citep{zhou2026}; and a checklist that
verifies cited assertions in narrative reviews is in development
\citep{vanra2026}.  Each asks whether evidence exists, supports a claim,
or suffices.  To our knowledge, none distinguishes claims from arguments
and propositions and applies a procedure suited to each.  That gap
matters most for papers whose main contribution is an argument.

% S1-6: AI-text detection is no substitute for verification (CLAIM, P2, SUPPORTED)
AI-text detection is no substitute: it asks who wrote a text, and
verification asks whether the text holds.  Detectors are also fragile:
their accuracy varies with generation settings \citep{dugan2024}, and
paraphrase can collapse it \citep{krishna2023}.

The failure modes described above arise in the writing process more than
in the AI model.  They occur when, as far as we know, nothing checks whether the language
of an argument or proposition matches its support, or whether an
argument is logically sound and not only well-sourced.  This paper asks what such
infrastructure might look like for arguments and propositions, and
whether existing scholarly frameworks provide a foundation for building
it.

\section{The Landscape Gap}

% S2-1: EQUATOR ~700 guidelines (CLAIM, P0, ESTABLISHED)
The most comprehensive verification infrastructure for academic writing
to date is the EQUATOR Network.  EQUATOR (Enhancing the QUAlity
and Transparency Of health Research) maintains a database of nearly 700
reporting guidelines for health research \citepalias{equator}.  These
guidelines, among them CONSORT for randomised controlled trials, STROBE
for observational studies, and PRISMA for systematic reviews
\citep{page2021}, give authors structured checklists to complete while preparing a manuscript.  The GoodReports tool \citep{struthers2021}
selects the guideline that fits a study type, which lowers the barrier
to adoption further.  A Cochrane review found that journals endorsing
CONSORT favoured completeness on 25 of 27 checklist items, significantly
so for five \citep{turner2012}, and incomplete reporting has been
identified as a major contributor to research waste
\citep{glasziou2014}.

% S2-2: No guidelines for non-empirical papers (CLAIM, P0, EMERGING)
Yet the EQUATOR database points to a structural gap.  Virtually all of
its nearly 700 guidelines address research that collects, analyses and
reports data.  Systematic reviews (PRISMA; \citealp{page2021}) are a
partial exception, since they synthesise primary data without generating
it.  Beyond them, to our knowledge, no consensus-based guideline of the
EQUATOR type exists for theoretical, perspective, or conceptual papers.
Conceptual papers lack ``commonly accepted templates''
\citep[p.~18]{jaakkola2020}.  Design-science researchers have reported no agreed guidance on
presenting and reviewing their papers \citep{gregor2013,dinter2015}, and
validity in the field
``is neither well-understood nor consistently established''
\citep{larsen2025}.  Software engineering's community standards come
closest: the one for methodological papers asks for ``clear, valid
arguments'' but gives no procedure for checking them \citep{ralph2020}.
The best-known narrative-review instrument is ``not a reporting
guideline'' \citep{baethge2019}, and guidelines are only now being
developed for concept analysis \citep{nashwan2026} and narrative reviews
\citep{vanra2026}.  None sets out how to verify arguments or
propositions as distinct from cited statements.

% S2-3: Gregor's theory types (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
This absence may reflect a difference in what needs verifying across
paper types.  \citet{gregor2006} identified five theory types in
information systems research, based on four goals: analysis,
explanation, prediction, and prescription.  Two of these types are
non-empirical in their primary contribution: Type~I (analytic theories,
including taxonomies and frameworks) and Type~V (design and action
theories, including design principles and methods), although Type~V
papers may include an empirical evaluation of the designed artefact.
Their core contributions are conceptual structures, which are valued
for clarity, coherence, and utility and cannot be checked against
measurements the way data points can.  The
remaining types (Type~II: explanatory, Type~III: predictive, Type~IV:
explanatory and predictive) fit empirical verification more naturally,
because they make claims about observable phenomena.

Gregor's taxonomy was developed for information systems, but the
underlying distinction applies more widely.  The IS community has
developed structured methodologies for non-empirical work, notably
\citeauthor{peffers2007}'s (\citeyear{peffers2007}) widely adopted
design science research process model, yet these methodologies have not
been turned into reporting guidelines analogous to EQUATOR.  Perspective
articles, commentaries, theoretical syntheses, and methodological
proposals share the same feature: their primary contribution is an
argument or a framework, not empirical evidence.  Such papers appear in
virtually every academic discipline, from the humanities and social
sciences to engineering and medicine.

% S2-4: Argument quality is primary challenge (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
For these non-empirical paper types, the central verification question
may be less ``do the data support the claims?'' and more ``is the
argument logically sound, are the premises established, and are the
boundary conditions specified?'' \citep{toulmin2003, whetten1989}.  That
is a different task from the one EQUATOR guidelines are designed to
support.

{\sloppy This suggests that the gap in EQUATOR's coverage is structural.
Empirical papers need verification of research design, data collection,
analysis, and reporting, and structured checklists suit those well.
Non-empirical papers need verification of argument quality: whether
warrants are valid, reasoning is coherent, confidence is calibrated, and
boundaries are specified.  These are different verification tasks that
may require different infrastructure.  The gap is most acute in purely
non-empirical papers, but it is a systemic risk wherever authors move
from data-driven claims to interpretive reasoning, including the
discussion sections of empirical research.  The question is whether
established frameworks from argumentation theory and theory-building
methodology can provide a foundation for this infrastructure.\par}

\section{Typed Verification: A Proposal}

We propose that verification infrastructure for non-empirical papers
should distinguish three unit types, each with its own verification
procedure (Table~\ref{tab:types}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Three verification unit types with per-type procedures.}
\label{tab:types}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Verification question}
  & \textbf{Framework} \\
\midrule
Claim       & Source-verifiable fact
  & Does the source say this?  & Citation checking \\
Argument    & Evidence + inference $\to$ conclusion
  & Is the reasoning valid?    & Toulmin (1958/2003) \\
Proposition & Recommendation with boundaries
  & Are conditions specified?  & Whetten (1989) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The first and most familiar type is the \textbf{claim}: a factual
statement that can be checked against one or more sources.  Claims are
the default unit in citation-based verification.  ``Study~X found~Y'' is
a claim, and its accuracy can be checked by reading Study~X.  Most
existing verification tools, including citation checkers and
fact-verification systems, work on claims.  For a claim the verification
question is simple: does the cited source exist, and does it say what
the manuscript attributes to it?

% S3-1: Toulmin for arguments (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
The second type is the \textbf{argument}: a chain of reasoning that
combines evidence with inference to reach a conclusion.  Arguments
appear throughout academic writing, particularly in discussion and
conclusion sections, yet they resist claim-level verification, because
their validity depends on the logical relation between premises and
conclusion and not on any single source.  \citet{toulmin2003} provides a
well-established framework for analysing argument structure.  It
decomposes an argument into six components: claim (the conclusion
asserted), grounds (the evidence offered), warrant (the inference that
connects grounds to claim), backing (support for the warrant), qualifier
(the degree of certainty), and rebuttal (conditions under which the
argument does not hold).  This framework may provide a basis for
verifying arguments in academic writing: an argument can be evaluated by
checking whether its grounds are established, its warrant is explicit and valid for the target audience, and its
qualifier
matches the strength of the evidence.  (Our operationalisation uses five
of the six components and treats backing as implicit when the target
audience already accepts the warrant.)  \citet{gupta2024} found that
LLMs can extract Toulmin-style argument structures through zero-shot
prompting, which suggests that automated support for argument-level
verification may be technically feasible.

% S3-2: Whetten for propositions (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
The third type is the \textbf{proposition}: a novel recommendation or
contribution that specifies what should be done and under what
conditions.  Propositions are the defining unit of many non-empirical
papers, because they are what such a paper contributes to its field.
\citet{whetten1989} provides a framework for evaluating theoretical
contributions through four components: What (the constructs or factors
involved), How (the relationships between them), Why (the underlying
logic), and Who/Where/When (the boundary conditions that say where the
contribution applies).  A proposition can be evaluated without empirical
data, by checking whether key constructs are defined, the reasoning is explicit,
and the boundary conditions are stated.

% S3-3: Confidence-to-language mapping (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
The three types may benefit from a shared confidence calibration system.
We propose mapping confidence tiers to prescribed language
(Table~\ref{tab:confidence}), so that the certainty expressed in the
text follows the strength of the underlying evidence.  This mapping may
let authors and reviewers detect confidence inflation, the failure mode
described in Section~1, during drafting,
before peer review.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Proposed confidence-to-language mapping.}
\label{tab:confidence}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Assign when\ldots}
  & \textbf{Prescribed language} \\
\midrule
Established & Multiple independent sources; no dispute
  & ``demonstrates,'' ``confirms'' \\
Supported   & 2--3 sources agree; open questions remain
  & ``indicates,'' ``evidence suggests'' \\
Emerging    & 1--2 sources; not yet replicated
  & ``may,'' ``preliminary evidence'' \\
Speculative & Logical inference; no direct data
  & ``warrants investigation'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

% S3-4: Different types need different verification (ARGUMENT, P0, EMERGING)
The central argument of this paper is that these three types require
fundamentally different verification procedures, and that applying the
wrong procedure to a type may produce systematic errors.  A claim
evaluated as an argument, by checking its warrant instead of its source,
wastes effort.  More consequentially, an argument evaluated as a claim,
by checking whether it has a source instead of whether its reasoning is
valid, may produce false failures: entries scored as unverified because
the procedure did not suit their unit type, not because they are wrong.
This argument rests on the premise that different epistemic
contributions (facts, inferences, recommendations) have different
validity criteria; if the premise holds, a single verification
procedure will systematically misclassify some entries.  One might ask whether a simpler split
between empirical and non-empirical verification would do.  The distinction
between arguments and propositions matters because they fail
differently: an argument with a weak warrant needs better reasoning, and
a proposition with missing boundary conditions needs better scoping.
Putting both under one ``non-empirical'' category hides which correction
is needed.

\section{Related Work and Design Rationale}

The framework described in Section~3 is proposed as a typed addition to
the approaches reviewed in Section~1.  An empirical evaluation across
several paper projects, authors, and domains is needed before general
claims about its effectiveness can be made.  Appendix~\ref{app:self}
gives one self-application data point: the verification registry kept
while this paper was written, which shows the framework applied
prospectively to a perspective paper.

% S4-3: External validation (CLAIM, P1, EMERGING)
The design draws on independent research on structured verification.
Results suggest that representing support and attack relations formally
improves LLM review aggregation over end-to-end approaches
\citep{peerarg2024}, and that prompting with Toulmin's model improves
LLM argument extraction over generic prompts \citep{gupta2024}.  Both
suggest that structured frameworks combined with LLMs may outperform
LLMs alone, which is consistent with the approach proposed here.

The proposal is offered as a model for the scholarly community to test
and adapt.  Whether typed verification adds real value across authors,
domains, and writing workflows remains an open empirical question.

\section{Call to Action}

% S5-1: The proposition (PROPOSITION, P0, EMERGING)
AI writing assistance creates verification challenges beyond citation
hallucination (Section~1), yet EQUATOR's nearly 700 reporting guidelines
address empirical research almost exclusively, which leaves
non-empirical paper types without systematic verification support
(Section~2).  On the basis of this gap, and of the argument that claims,
arguments, and propositions each need their own verification procedure
(Section~3), we propose that the scholarly community develop
verification infrastructure for non-empirical papers, with procedures
differentiated by unit type:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Source verification} for claims (does the cited source say
  this?);
\item \textbf{Toulmin-based analysis} for arguments (is the reasoning
  valid, are the premises established, is the qualifier calibrated?);
\item \textbf{Whetten-based analysis} for propositions (are the boundary
  conditions specified, is the underlying logic explicit?).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Each type would have its own checklist, confidence calibration
criteria, and language guidelines that authors can apply while preparing a manuscript.

This proposal has specific boundary conditions.  It applies to academic
papers intended for peer-reviewed publication in fields where argument
quality is a primary contribution.  It does not apply to informal
writing, journalism, creative writing, or very short opinion pieces,
where the overhead of formal verification would exceed the benefit.  The
verification frameworks proposed here, Toulmin's argument model and
Whetten's framework for theoretical contributions, are grounded in
Western academic argumentation traditions.  Their fit with scholarly
traditions that have different rhetorical conventions would need to be
assessed, not assumed.

One might object that journal peer review already verifies the arguments
of non-empirical papers, since reviewers routinely evaluate logical
coherence, boundary conditions, and engagement with counter-arguments.
The objection has merit, and peer review remains essential.  AI writing
assistance may, however, stretch the verification challenge beyond what
peer review was designed to handle.  When AI tools can produce fluent,
well-structured prose with little effort, the volume and apparent
quality of submitted manuscripts may rise while the share of
well-reasoned arguments among them falls.  Reviewers already face growing workloads;
detecting AI-generated confidence inflation and implicit warrant
failures as well may exceed what the system can absorb.  Process-level verification infrastructure would complement peer review,
not replace it: it would catch argument-level problems before
submission, so that reviewers can concentrate on substantive evaluation.

% S5-2: Next steps (CLAIM, P2, SPECULATIVE)
Several directions warrant investigation.  First, the typed verification
model proposed here has been developed and applied within medical
simulation and instrumentation.  Discipline-specific verification
templates, which adapt the three-type model to the conventions of other
fields, would be needed to assess how far the framework generalises.
Second, integration with AI writing tools could enable verification
during drafting, with the checklists and calibration
criteria serving as prompts for writing assistants.  Third, an empirical comparison of the writing quality of papers written
with and without typed verification would provide the evidence needed to move
the proposal from preliminary to supported.  Fourth, although this paper
focuses on non-empirical paper types, the discussion sections of
empirical papers make the same move from data to interpretation, and may
benefit from the same typed procedures applied to that step.  Fifth, the
calculation errors described in Section~1, where AI-generated equations
produce plausible but incorrect results, suggest that numerical
reproduction (mechanically recomputing derived values from stated
inputs) may warrant recognition as a distinct verification procedure,
next to source checking, argument analysis, and proposition evaluation.

The EQUATOR Network has spent two decades building nearly 700 reporting
guidelines for empirical health research, and these guidelines have
measurably improved the quality and transparency of published research
\citep{turner2012, glasziou2014}.  We know of no consensus-based
equivalent for non-empirical papers: theoretical contributions, design
science research, perspective articles, and conceptual papers.  As AI
writing tools make fluent academic prose easier to produce, the gap
between what is published and what is rigorously verified may widen.
We suggest it is time to start building verification infrastructure for
these papers too.

\paragraph{Proposition (S5-1).}
``The scholarly community should develop verification infrastructure for
non-empirical papers.''  This is the paper's single proposition.  Its
Whetten decomposition: \emph{What:} typed verification infrastructure
that distinguishes claims, arguments, and propositions.  \emph{How:}
per-type checklists adapted from Toulmin (arguments) and Whetten
(propositions), with a confidence-to-language mapping.  \emph{Why:}
EQUATOR provides nearly 700 guidelines for empirical research (S2-1),

AI writing amplifies the verification challenge.  \emph{Boundary

publication; excludes informal writing, journalism, and creative
writing; the proposed checklists are grounded in Western argumentation
traditions and may need adaptation for other scholarly conventions.

\subsection*{Corrections discovered during verification}

The verification process caught two kinds of issue before submission:
(1)~the EQUATOR guideline count was corrected from approximately 500 to
nearly 700 (S2-1); and (2)~the citation hallucination claim (S1-1) at
first lacked a source, which prompted a literature search that found
\citet{mugaanyi2024}.  These corrections, from a factual error to
missing evidence, are the kinds of issue the framework is designed to
