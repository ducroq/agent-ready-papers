## Long version (blog, ~600 words)

### Writing that answers back

About 2,400 years ago, Plato had Socrates tell a story about the invention of writing.
The god Theuth brings his new art to King Thamus and says it will make people wiser and
improve their memory. Thamus turns it down. Writing, he says, will produce forgetfulness,
and it offers "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom" (*Phaedrus* 275a).

Socrates then adds a complaint that I find sharper. Written words, he says, are like
paintings: "if one asks them a question, they preserve a solemn silence." You might think
they speak with intelligence, "but if you question them … they always say only one and
the same thing" (275d). A text cannot defend itself, and it cannot explain what it meant.

Swap "writing" for "large language models" and the first worry sounds familiar. The
complaint about AI-assisted writing is the same: it gives the look of understanding
without the understanding, for the reader and sometimes for the writer too.

The second worry fits less well. In a sense, an LLM is writing that does
answer back. Ask it a question and it responds, at length, in fluent prose. The trouble is
that it can sound just as sure when it is wrong. Socrates wanted
text that could be questioned, and we now have it. What we lack is the habit of
questioning it, and of questioning our own work with it.

### From writer to questioner

Much advice on using LLMs for writing treats them as producers: draft this, polish that.
The tradition Socrates came from points the other way. Plato's dialogues, and Aristotle's
dialectic after them, test a claim by asking questions of it. What does it rest on? Does
that support actually carry it? Is it stated more confidently than the support allows?

Those questions do not need a philosopher to ask them. They can be put to a draft one
claim at a time, and an LLM is a patient, tireless partner for asking them. You stay the
one who decides whether an answer holds up. Used this way, the tool exercises your
judgement instead of replacing it.

### Making the questions explicit

In the 1950s the philosopher Stephen Toulmin gave these questions a structure. Every
argument, in his layout, has a claim, the grounds it rests on, a warrant linking the two,
and a qualifier saying how strongly the claim is put. That layout is the basis of a paper
I have just put out for feedback, *The Verification Gap: Checking the Reasoning in
AI-Assisted Academic Writing*.

Its starting point is that checking citations, that a cited work exists and says what it
is cited for, is not enough. A paper can pass that check and still fail: a claim can rest
on real evidence that does not carry it, or be worded more certainly than its support
allows. The paper proposes a claim register: each claim written down with what it rests
on, each step from grounds to claim checked, and each claim's wording tied to how well it
is supported. The paper applies the register to itself, and the result is not flattering:
three of its six central claims stay at a tentative level (at the time of writing), for reasons it names.

I should be clear about what the paper does not do. It does not test whether a register
helps authors or reviewers find weak reasoning. That needs a trial, and I would like to
hear from anyone interested in running one.

Plato worried that writing would make us look wise without being wise. With LLMs the
same risk is back. But a text that answers back is also a sparring partner Socrates never had.
Whether it makes us sharper or lazier depends on who asks the questions.

## Short version (LinkedIn, ~200 words)

In Plato's Phaedrus, King Thamus warns that writing gives "the appearance of wisdom,
not true wisdom." Socrates adds a sharper complaint: written words can't answer back. Question them and "they
always say only one and the same thing."

LLMs are, in a sense, writing that does answer back. The catch: they answer just as
confidently when they're wrong.

So maybe the useful question isn't "should we let AI write?" but "who is asking the
questions?" Plato's and Aristotle's way of testing a claim was to question it: What does
it rest on? Does that evidence carry it? Is it stated more strongly than the support
allows? An LLM can be a tireless partner for asking those questions of your own draft,
with you as the judge of the answers.

I've just shared a paper that turns those questions into a checklist for academic
writing: a claim register built on Toulmin's layout of arguments. Each claim is recorded
with what it rests on, and its wording is tied to how well it is supported. Applied to
itself, the paper (for now) leaves three of its six central claims at a tentative level.

Whether this helps people catch weak reasoning hasn't been tested yet. If you'd like to
try it, or tear it apart, I'd love to hear from you.
